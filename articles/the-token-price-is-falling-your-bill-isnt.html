The Token Price Is Falling. Your Bill Isn't.

Per-token prices drop about 10x a year, but agents multiply the tokens per task faster. The unit that predicts your bill is cost per completed task.

  • unit-economics
  • agentic-systems
  • pricing
  • strategy

On June 16, 2025, Cursor changed its $20-a-month Pro plan from a limit on requests to a limit on compute. Pro now included $20 of frontier-model usage at API prices, plus "unlimited" use of an automatic model picker. Many users read "unlimited" as covering the models they actually used. Surprise bills followed. On July 4, Cursor apologized, acknowledged that the rollout had hurt user trust, and offered refunds for unexpected charges since mid-June.[1][2]

Three weeks later, Anthropic announced weekly usage limits for Claude Code subscribers, effective August 28. It said some users ran the tool "continuously in the background, 24/7," and that one user consumed tens of thousands of dollars of model usage on a $200 plan.[3]

The easy reading is that these companies mispriced their plans. Both mispriced the same thing in the same way. Each sold access as if a request were a stable unit of cost. Agents broke that assumption.

Per-token prices fall roughly 10x a year for constant capability. Agentic workloads raise tokens per task faster than that. The number that predicts your bill, and your margin if you resell AI, is cost per completed task, not cost per token.

Two Curves

The falling curve is real. Andreessen Horowitz estimates that for a model of equivalent capability, inference cost has fallen about 10x per year. GPT-3-level performance went from $60 to $0.06 per million tokens in three years.[4] In July 2024, OpenAI launched GPT-4o mini at $0.15 per million input tokens and $0.60 per million output tokens, and said it beat the original GPT-4 on chat preferences.[5]

The rising curve is less visible. A chat answer is one model call. A retrieval-augmented answer adds retrieved context to every call. An agent loops: plan, call a tool, read the result, re-plan, retry on error. Every loop re-sends a growing context. Reasoning models add tokens you never see. OpenAI's documentation says reasoning tokens are billed as output tokens even though the API doesn't return them.[6]

Pause and predict: on the ARC-AGI benchmark in December 2024, how much more per task did OpenAI's o3 cost to go from 75.7% to 87.5%?

About 175 times more. The ARC Prize team reported about $26 per task for the 75.7% configuration and about $4,560 per task for the 87.5% configuration, which used 172 times more compute.[7] At the frontier, extra capability is bought mostly with extra tokens per task.

Put the curves together and you get tokens-per-task inflation. If the price per token falls 10x while tokens per task rise 50x as you move from chat to an agentic workflow, cost per task rises 5x. Your price sheet shows the first number. Your invoice shows the third.

Why Flat Prices Break

Seat-based and request-based pricing assume that heavy users cost a bit more than light ones. With chat, that held. Agents turn it into a power law. One developer's "request" might be a two-hour autonomous refactor that reads the whole repository several times over. Anthropic's "less than 5% of subscribers" affected by its new limits is that tail, made visible.[3]

The same physics applies inside a company. Your AI line item grows even as unit prices fall, because your teams use each price cut to move up a tier: from chat to RAG, from RAG to agents, from agents to reasoning agents. That isn't waste. Each step usually does more useful work. But it means the budget conversation has to happen in tasks, because tokens no longer predict anything.

The price sheet shows cost per token. The invoice shows cost per task. Only one of them is falling.

The Objection

The strongest counterargument

This is a temporary squeeze. Prices fall 10x a year. Whatever an agent costs today, the same agent will cost a tenth as much next year. Optimizing cost per task now is premature, so ship capability and let the price curve catch up.

That works for a fixed workload, and a16z's 10x figure is explicitly for constant capability.[4] Nobody's workload stays fixed. The teams that benefit from next year's price cut will spend it on longer tasks, bigger contexts, and more reasoning, because their competitors will. Waiting for the curve treats a moving target as a still one.

Part of the objection is right: don't over-optimize the first version of an agent. But measuring isn't optimizing. You can't make a build-vs-buy call, price a product, or notice a runaway loop without cost per completed task, and it's cheap to instrument.

Four Moves Monday

Three Things to Carry

  • Token prices fall for constant capability. Your capability isn't constant.
  • Agents turn one request into many calls. Price and budget in completed tasks.
  • Cheaper per token can be dearer per task once you count the retries.

Check Your Understanding

Why did both Cursor and Anthropic have to change plans within weeks of each other?

Both had priced access per request or per seat. Agentic use made cost per request extremely variable, so a small share of users consumed a large share of compute.

Model A costs $1/M tokens with 50% success. Model B costs $1.80/M with 95% success, same tokens per attempt. Which is cheaper per task?

Model B. A: 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2.0. B: 1.8 ÷ 0.95 ≈ 1.9 (in units of price × tokens). And B also returns fewer failures to a person.

What does the ARC-AGI o3 result say about buying capability at the frontier?

That the last points of accuracy can cost orders of magnitude more per task, about 175x for roughly 12 points there. Whether that's worth it depends on the value of a completed task, which is why that's the unit to measure.
