The Leaderboard Is a Press Release

A public benchmark score now measures a vendor's release process as much as its model. The only eval that predicts your production is the one built from your traffic.

  • evaluation
  • benchmarks
  • vendor-critique
  • model-selection

In early April 2025, Meta launched Llama 4, and one variant, Maverick, appeared near the top of LMArena, the crowd-voted chatbot leaderboard that many buyers treat as a scoreboard. Within days, people noticed that the model on the leaderboard wasn't the model Meta released. It was "Llama-4-Maverick-03-26-Experimental," a version that Meta itself described as optimized for conversationality. LMArena apologized, changed its policies, and added the released model. The vanilla Maverick ranked below GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Gemini 1.5 Pro, all older models.[1][2]

The obvious reading: one vendor gamed one leaderboard, and the leaderboard tightened its rules. The deeper problem showed up three weeks later, in a paper titled The Leaderboard Illusion.

A public benchmark score now measures a vendor's release process as much as its model. The only evaluation that predicts your production behavior is one you build from your own traffic, run yourself, and keep private.

Selection, Not Cheating

The Leaderboard Illusion authors, from Cohere Labs, Princeton, Stanford, Ai2, and others, documented how the Arena actually worked. A handful of providers could test many private variants before a release and publish only the best score. Before Llama 4, Meta tested 27 private variants. Proprietary models were also sampled more often and removed less often. Google and OpenAI received an estimated 19.2% and 20.4% of all Arena battle data, which is free training signal for the exact distribution the leaderboard measures.[3]

Nobody needs to cheat for this to inflate scores. Testing 27 variants and picking the top one is best-of-N on a noisy measurement. Even if every variant had identical underlying quality, the maximum of 27 noisy scores sits well above the average. The leaderboard then records a fact about how many draws you took.

Same Shape, Three Benchmarks

The Arena is a human-preference benchmark, but the same mechanism shows up in static tests.

Contamination. In May 2024, Scale AI researchers wrote GSM1k: 1,000 new grade-school math problems matched in style and difficulty to the famous GSM8k test. Several model families scored up to 8% lower on the new problems. Models that were more likely to reproduce GSM8k examples word for word showed bigger gaps, which suggests partial memorization.[4] The test had been in the training data, so the score partly measured exposure.

Broken instruments. In August 2024, OpenAI published SWE-bench Verified after paying 93 developers to review 1,699 tasks from the original SWE-bench. They removed 68.3% of the reviewed tasks as underspecified, unfair, or ungradable.[5] For a year before that, the industry had compared coding agents on a benchmark where most of the reviewed tasks were flawed. The leaderboard looked precise. The ruler was bent.

Pause and predict: what do private variants, contamination, and broken tasks have in common?

In each case, the score was optimized against, or polluted by, the same public distribution it claims to measure. Selection (best-of-N), leakage (training on the test), and instrument error (unfair tasks) all push the headline number away from what you'll see on your own work. Only the first one involves anyone choosing to do it.

This is Goodhart's law with a procurement budget attached. Once a public number decides funding rounds, launch coverage, and enterprise shortlists, everything upstream bends toward that number: which variant ships, what goes into training, which tasks the eval team prioritizes. You can call it the selection tax. It's the gap between the published score and what the model will do on data nobody optimized for.

A leaderboard rank tells you how many times the vendor tried. Your eval tells you whether it works.

The Objection

The strongest counterargument

Benchmarks still track real capability. The GSM1k authors themselves found that frontier models showed minimal overfitting and that all models generalized to new problems. Arena rankings broadly agree with what practitioners experience. A noisy public signal beats no signal, and most teams don't have the resources to build serious evals.

That's right at coarse grain. Benchmarks reliably separate a 2023 model from a 2026 one, and the GSM1k paper says so.[4] But procurement decisions are rarely coarse. They're choices between three frontier models whose public scores sit within a few points of each other. That's exactly the range where the selection tax is as large as the differences being compared. The signal is real across generations and unreliable within one.

"We don't have the resources" is also the wrong cost comparison. A useful private eval is small: a couple of hundred real, anonymized requests from your logs with graded expected outcomes. That costs a few engineer-days. Picking the wrong model for a system you'll run for two years costs much more.

Four Moves Monday

Three Things to Carry

  • Best-of-N on a noisy test produces a winner even when nothing improved.
  • Public benchmarks are good across generations and unreliable within one, which is where your decision sits.
  • The eval that matters is small, private, built from your traffic, and nobody gets to tune against it.

The score was optimized against the same distribution it claims to measure. That's not a measurement anymore; it's marketing.

Check Your Understanding

Why can private testing inflate scores even if no variant is better than the others?

Because publishing the maximum of N noisy measurements is biased upward. The expected maximum grows with N and with noise, independent of true skill.

Which of the three mechanisms (selection, contamination, broken tasks) can your own private eval also suffer from?

All three. If you tune prompts against it, you get selection. If its items leak into fine-tuning data, you get contamination. If items have unclear "correct" answers, you get a broken instrument. Hence the held-out split and the quarterly refresh.

When is a public leaderboard genuinely useful?

For coarse filtering, such as which models are even worth evaluating, and for tracking capability across generations. Not for choosing between close competitors.
