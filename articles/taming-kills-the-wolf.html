The Obedient Wolf

On January 23, 2025, OpenAI released Operator — a Computer Using Agent that could browse the web, click buttons, fill forms, and complete tasks on your behalf. Under the hood, GPT-4o could reason about what to do on a webpage, plan a sequence of actions, and execute them. The model was capable.

The deployment was not.

Operator launched restricted to a curated list of roughly 50 websites. It refused to interact with pages containing certain content categories. It paused mid-task to request human confirmation before taking action. It timed out on anything complex. The model inside Operator could have booked your flight; the product called Operator mostly asked if you were sure you wanted it to try.

This wasn't a bug. It was the design. [BEAT 1]

Every Leash Cuts Both Ways

Enterprise AI deployment is a domestication process, not an engineering challenge. Every guardrail and safety layer, every human-in-the-loop checkpoint is a selection pressure — a filter that lets some traits through and blocks others. In evolutionary biology, domestication works by applying consistent selection pressure for a narrow set of traits: tameness, tolerance of proximity, compliance with handling. The result is predictable. You get an organism that's safe to keep around. You also get an organism that's lost the capabilities you originally valued.

Belyaev's fox domestication experiment proved this concretely. Starting in 1959, Dmitry Belyaev selected silver foxes for a single trait: tameness. Within a few generations, the foxes had floppy ears, curled tails, piebald coats, and reduced skull size. No one selected for floppy ears. They came along for free, because biological traits are linked — you can't select for tameness without getting the package it ships with. [QUOTE 1]

The same linkage operates in AI systems. You can't select for "won't cause harm" without also selecting against the capability to act autonomously in ambiguous situations. You can't select for "always asks before acting" without also selecting against the ability to handle tasks that require forty intermediate decisions without human input. The traits are linked, and the linkage runs one direction: compliance costs capability, but capability doesn't guarantee compliance. [QUOTE 5]

Same Crate, Different Dog

This isn't an OpenAI problem. It's a structural problem that operates at every layer of the stack simultaneously.

Model layer — Claude

Constitutional AI trains for helpful + harmless. Practitioners report Claude refusing legitimate tool-use — explaining the task, then declining to execute. Refusal reflex as proxy for safety, overshooting the target.

Product layer — Copilot

IT-controlled restrictions block financial data, document modification, cross-app search. Forrester: only a third of users rate it "very useful." Model can reason across data; deployment prevents it.

Org layer — Agentforce

Marketed as "autonomous agents." Ships as scoped workflows, ~5–10 pre-approved actions. Enterprise liability breeds out autonomy. Same result, different kennel.

At the model layer, RLHF and constitutional AI training are selection pressures. Anthropic's constitutional AI explicitly trains Claude to be helpful and harmless — but practitioners throughout February 2025 reported Claude refusing legitimate tool-use requests, explaining what it could do and then declining to do it. The training selected for a refusal reflex as a proxy for safety, and the proxy overshot. Claude could describe the task, outline the steps, and then refuse to execute — a model that knew exactly what to do and wouldn't do it.

At the product layer, guardrails are selection pressures. Microsoft Copilot entered enterprise deployments with IT-controlled restrictions that prevented access to financial data, blocked document modification, and disabled cross-application search. Forrester's enterprise survey found that only a third of Copilot users rated it "very useful" in these configurations. The model could reason across your organization's data. The deployment prevented it.

At the organization layer, procurement and liability frameworks are selection pressures. Salesforce's Agentforce hit general availability in February 2025, marketing "autonomous AI agents" while delivering tightly scoped workflows with AI-assisted decision points — limited to roughly five to ten pre-approved action types per deployment. The marketing said "autonomous." The product said "ask permission first." Enterprise liability was the selection pressure, and it produced the same result as Belyaev's fox experiment: an organism bred for tameness, carrying traits no one asked for.

We didn't fail to build autonomous agents. We succeeded in building obedient ones. [QUOTE 2] — the perspective shift

[BEAT 2]

The Bite We Breed Out

The strongest counterargument is obvious and partially correct: guardrails prevent real harm. An AI agent that can book flights can also book flights to the wrong city with your company card. An agent that can modify documents can also delete the wrong database. Safety isn't optional.

But the counterargument misses what's invisible: the cost of guardrails goes unmeasured because no one tracks capability loss. We measure false-positive rates on safety filters. We count prevented incidents. We don't count the tasks the system could have completed but didn't — because counterfactuals don't show up in dashboards. The principal-agent problem between IT (who controls guardrails and is evaluated on risk reduction) and users (who need capability and are evaluated on output) guarantees over-domestication. IT's incentive is to prevent every possible harm. No one has the incentive to measure the harms of over-prevention. [QUOTE 3]

Domestication doesn't ask which traits you meant to keep. It keeps whichever traits survive the culling.

Gartner projected that through 2025, at least 30% of generative AI projects will be abandoned after proof of concept. That statistic is usually read as "the technology isn't ready." Read it instead as: the domestication process is working exactly as expected, and what it's producing isn't what anyone wanted.

Here's a useful frame: if you can name the selection pressure, you can see what it's selecting for. Most organizations can't name theirs — which means they're running a breeding program without knowing what they're breeding.

Train for the Hunt

Three concrete actions that change the selection pressure:

Measure capability, not just compliance. Track what users accomplish with the AI tool, not just what the tool is prevented from doing. If your deployment metrics are 100% safety metrics, you have no way to know whether your guardrails are costing more than they save. Add "tasks completed successfully" as a first-class metric with the same weight as "incidents prevented." [QUOTE 4]

Make the principal-agent problem visible. The people who set guardrails and the people who use the AI tool have different incentives. Name this explicitly in your organization. Give users control over their own guardrail levels with informed consent — let them opt into higher capability at higher risk, the way we let developers opt into root access. The current model, where IT sets a single threshold for the entire organization, keeps every dog on the same length leash regardless of breed or task.

Run wild-capability audits. Periodically test what the underlying model can do without guardrails, in a sandboxed environment. You need to know what you're selecting against. If you've never seen the un-domesticated version of your AI system, you can't know how much capability you've bred out. Belyaev kept a control population of undomesticated foxes alongside his breeding line — the contrast between wild and tame was what made the results legible.

Check the Collar

Open the guardrail configuration for your most-deployed AI tool. Count the "prevent" rules versus the "enable" rules. If the ratio exceeds 3:1, you are running a domestication program, not a deployment. Name it. Then ask what capabilities your users have been quietly working around — because they always are, and what they're using instead is almost certainly less safe than the AI system you over-guarded.

Test Your Tracking

What did Belyaev's fox experiment prove about selecting for a single trait?
Selecting for tameness alone produced a cascade of unintended changes — floppy ears, reduced skulls, lost problem-solving — because traits are linked. You don't get to pick which ones come along.
Why do guardrails cost capability, not just add safety?
Compliance and capability are linked traits. Selecting for "won't cause harm" necessarily selects against "can act in ambiguity." The linkage runs one direction: compliance costs capability, but capability doesn't guarantee compliance.
What makes over-domestication invisible in organizations?
We measure prevented incidents, not tasks that could have been completed. IT (evaluated on risk) and users (evaluated on output) have misaligned incentives, so no one holds the incentive to measure the cost of over-prevention.
