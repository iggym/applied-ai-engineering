Approval Is Not Oversight

A human who approves AI output by default soon becomes a signature. Oversight works only when reviewers are measured on what they catch, not what they pass.

  • human-in-the-loop
  • governance
  • incident-analysis
  • org-design

On the night of March 18, 2018, an Uber test vehicle driving itself in Tempe, Arizona, struck and killed a pedestrian. A human safety operator was behind the wheel to take over if the automation failed. The National Transportation Safety Board found that the operator had been visually distracted by a personal phone for much of the trip. It also named contributing factors at the company level: Uber's inadequate safety risk assessment, ineffective oversight of vehicle operators, and "a lack of adequate mechanisms for addressing operators' automation complacency."[1]

The obvious reading puts the blame on one inattentive person. The NTSB didn't stop there, and neither should we. The operator was the "human in the loop." The system around the operator assumed that a human in the seat equals oversight. It made no effort to keep that human alert, test them, or catch the moment they stopped looking.

Human review that approves AI output by default soon becomes a signature. Oversight works only when the reviewer is tested, sampled, and measured on what they catch, not on how fast they approve.

Signatures Without Review

Generative AI has made the Tempe pattern cheap and common. The outputs are fluent, usually right, and fast to approve.

The brief. In Mata v. Avianca, a personal-injury case in federal court in New York, the plaintiff's lawyers filed a brief citing cases that ChatGPT had invented, complete with fake quotes and internal citations. When opposing counsel couldn't find the cases, the court ordered copies. On June 22, 2023, Judge P. Kevin Castel fined the lawyers $5,000.[2] A licensed attorney signed every page. The signature certified a review that never happened.

The reading list. In May 2025, the Chicago Sun-Times and The Philadelphia Inquirer ran a syndicated summer guide produced by King Features. Its reading list recommended books by real authors that don't exist, such as a novel by Andy Weir called The Last Algorithm. Most of the recommended books on the list were made up. The freelance writer admitted not checking the chatbot's output. The writer, the syndicate, and two newspapers all passed it along.[3]

The report. In 2025, Deloitte Australia delivered a A$440,000 independent review to the federal Department of Employment and Workplace Relations. A University of Sydney researcher found that it cited non-existent academic papers and included a quote attributed to a Federal Court judgment that the judgment doesn't contain. Deloitte acknowledged using a GPT-4o-based tool in drafting and agreed to repay the final installment of the contract.[4]

Pause and predict: how many layers of review did the fake reading list pass through?

At least four: the writer, the syndicate's editors, and two newspapers' production processes. Each layer assumed another layer had checked. Adding approvals didn't add oversight. It spread the responsibility thin enough that nobody held it.

These aren't isolated. A public database maintained by the legal researcher Damien Charlotin had logged more than 1,600 court decisions involving AI-hallucinated content in filings by mid-2026.[5][6] Each filing carried a professional's signature.

    The planted errors are the kind found in real incidents: a fabricated pinpoint quote, and a case that doesn't exist. The lesson isn't your score. Errors that look like the surrounding correct text are only caught by checking against a source, never by reading for plausibility.

    Why Reviewers Fall Asleep

    The mechanism is old, and the NTSB named it: automation complacency. When an automated system is right most of the time, people watching it gradually shift from checking to trusting. This isn't a moral failure. It's a rational response to feedback: every check that finds nothing teaches the reviewer that checking is a waste of time. Generative AI makes this worse in two ways. Its errors are fluent, looking exactly like its correct output. And its output arrives in volume, so review time per item shrinks.

    That turns many "human-in-the-loop" policies into rituals. A queue with an approve button, a target throughput, and no measurement of misses doesn't produce oversight. It produces signatures: a person whose name certifies work they weren't set up to check.

    A reviewer who has never been tested on a planted error is a signature, not a safeguard.

    The Objection

    The strongest counterargument

    These are failures of professional diligence, not of system design. The lawyers, the writer, and the consultants were already obliged to check their work, and they didn't. Sanctions, firings, and refunds are the system working. Adding seeded errors and review metrics means treating professionals like airport baggage screeners.

    Every one of those people had a professional obligation, and it didn't help. An obligation creates a penalty after a failure. It doesn't create a mechanism that prevents one. The NTSB's finding is useful here because it refused to stop at the individual: the operator was distracted, and the organization had no mechanism to address the complacency it should have expected.[1]

    The objection is right that measurement can turn insulting or performative. The answer is to measure the process rather than rank the people: catch rates on seeded items, review time against item complexity, and how often reviewers check sources. Professions that take vigilance seriously already do this. Treating it as beneath us is how a signature ends up on a fabricated citation.

    Four Moves Monday

    Three Things to Carry

    • A human in the seat isn't oversight. A human who gets tested is.
    • Fluent errors are caught by checking sources, not by reading for plausibility.
    • More approvers spread responsibility. One named reviewer holds it.

    Check Your Understanding

    Why did the NTSB's Uber finding go beyond the distracted operator?

    Because complacency is predictable. An organization that puts a human in the loop and does nothing to sustain their attention has designed for the failure it got.

    Why don't extra layers of approval fix the reading-list failure?

    Each layer reasonably assumes another layer checked the facts. Without one named person responsible for verification against sources, more layers spread responsibility rather than adding scrutiny.

    What do seeded errors measure that throughput metrics can't?

    Whether reviewers actually catch the kind of error the system produces. Throughput measures how fast approvals happen. Catch rate measures whether the oversight exists at all.
