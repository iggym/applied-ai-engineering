Renting Your Own Moat

Vendor absorbs your roadmap You own your abstraction layer

From paying rent on someone else's moat to building your own — the spectrum isn't cheap-vs-expensive, it's dependent-vs-compounding.

In March 2025, Klarna published the number that stopped the procurement world: 1,200 SaaS vendors consolidated to roughly 25. Annual savings: $13 million. The fintech press called it austerity. The AI engineering press called it an AI story. Both missed what actually happened.

Each of those 1,200 vendors represented a decision Klarna had outsourced to someone else's roadmap. A function the company no longer performed. An integration it couldn't untangle without risk, because the vendor's API had become the shape of Klarna's own process. A data flow it didn't control, flowing through someone else's infrastructure, subject to someone else's deprecation schedule.

The thirteen million wasn't the story. The story was capability recovery. Every vendor function that moved internal became something Klarna could change in days instead of negotiating for months. The cost savings were a lagging indicator. The leading indicator was that Klarna stopped renting the components of its own operation and started owning them.

Now extend that pattern to AI infrastructure, where the dependency dynamics run deeper. The vendor doesn't just host your data. They host your model's behavior — its outputs, its failure modes, its quirks. They host your prompt library, your evaluation criteria, your error handling. Your entire reasoning pipeline lives inside someone else's abstraction, and they can deprecate any layer of it with ninety days' notice.

The Cover Charge

The industry frames build-versus-buy for AI as a simple cost comparison: per-token API pricing on one side, GPU amortization and ML engineering salaries on the other. That framing omits the actual profit center.

Per-token pricing covers compute cost and customer acquisition. It's designed to get you integrated fast and deep. The margin comes later, from the switching cost that accumulates invisibly once your prompt engineering, your eval suites, your error handling, and your feature flags are all coupled to one model's specific behaviors. [BEAT 1]

The per-token price is the cover charge, not the bill.

OpenAI's deprecation cycle made this concrete in the first quarter of 2025. When model versions were sunset — gpt-4 base, multiple gpt-3.5-turbo variants — enterprises running production systems on those models received ninety-day migration windows. Each production use case required re-prompting, re-evaluation, and regression testing against the new model's behavior. Practitioners on the OpenAI community forum reported two to six weeks of engineering work per use case. A company with eight production deployments — a modest footprint — faced three to twelve months of unplanned migration labor. That cost never appears in any pricing sheet. It's priced into the dependency itself.

Dependency Rent

There's a term for this in systems thinking: dependency rent. It's the ongoing cost of not owning a layer your system depends on. You pay it in three forms. Integration coupling — the engineering hours already spent adapting your system to the vendor's interface, which must be re-spent when the interface changes. Capability atrophy — the skills your team no longer practices because the vendor handles that function, which means you can't perform it yourself when you need to. Roadmap mismatch — the vendor builds what serves their growth, not yours, and you adapt your product to their platform's capabilities instead of the reverse.

Klarna experienced all three forms at once. The vendor consolidation was a big procurement decision with a clean payoff.

Klarna experienced all three forms at once. The vendor consolidation wasn't a procurement decision; it was a dependency extraction. And extraction is always more expensive than the original integration, because the vendor has had years to make leaving hard. [BEAT 2]

Klarna didn't save thirteen million dollars by switching vendors — they saved it by becoming their own.

The Compounding Line

Klarna's vendor story and the OpenAI deprecation story look like separate events. They're the same mechanism at different timescales. Klarna hit the late-stage cost of accumulated dependency — the moment when switching costs are so high that you either keep paying rent or mount a painful extraction. The deprecation cycle is the early-stage version: each model version you adopt weaves another strand into the dependency web, adding to the switching cost you'll eventually face.

Every quarter you stay on a vendor's abstraction layer is a quarter you didn't invest in your own.

This is the compounding asymmetry the build-versus-buy debate usually ignores. Vendor spend is an operating expense that maintains your current position. Internal platform investment is a capital expense that compounds. The organization that owns its AI abstraction layer can swap models in days, not months. It can run experiments the vendor wouldn't prioritize. It can keep its prompt engineering and evaluation logic intact when a model gets deprecated, because those assets live in its own repository, not in a vendor's configuration panel.

The principal-agent problem is structural. The vendor's incentive is to make integration deep and switching expensive — that's where margin lives after the initial customer-acquisition pricing expires. Databricks' $1.3 billion acquisition of MosaicML wasn't a bet on training infrastructure efficiency. It was a bet that owning the training layer creates stickiness that per-token pricing alone can't deliver. The same logic explains why every major AI vendor is bundling evaluation tooling, deployment infrastructure, and fine-tuning pipelines into their platform. Each bundle is another coupling layer. Each layer makes leaving harder.

The strongest counterargument is real: most organizations lack the engineering capacity to build and maintain an internal AI platform, and the talent that exists is expensive. This is true for the first year. The gateway abstraction layer that enterprises adopted in early 2025 changes the intermediate calculus. Organizations running a gateway pattern — the LiteLLM or Portkey architecture — reduced model-switching time from approximately three weeks to roughly two days. The gateway doesn't replace the vendor. It makes vendor choice reversible. And reversibility is what turns a dependency into a commodity.

Four Levers

What shifts the calculus within a single quarter:

Audit your coupling. Count the production systems where a single vendor's model deprecation would trigger an unplanned migration. If the number exceeds two, you're already paying dependency rent — it just doesn't appear on any invoice.

Insert an abstraction layer now. A gateway proxy costs two to four engineering weeks to stand up. A gateway doesn't make inference cheaper; it makes the next migration nearly free. That's the cost event you're actually optimizing for, even if it hasn't happened yet.

A gateway doesn't make inference cheaper; it makes the next migration nearly free.

Own your evals. Vendor-provided evaluation benchmarks are optimized to show vendor models favorably. Goodhart's law, applied to marketing decks. Build your own evaluation suite against your own production data. This is the asset that survives any model migration, and it's the one vendors most want you to depend on them for.

Price the option value. When comparing build versus buy, add a line item for the cost of the next forced migration — estimated at two to six weeks of engineering time per production use case, times the number of use cases, times the probability of a deprecation event within eighteen months. That single number changes the decision in most cases.

The vendor's roadmap is not your strategy, but most organizations can't tell the difference until the deprecation notice arrives.
